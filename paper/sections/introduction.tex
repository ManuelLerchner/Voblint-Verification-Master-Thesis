\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A context-sensitive static analyzer can distinguish abstract states for
different calling contexts at the same program point. In the program of \cref{fig:running},
\texttt{main} calls \texttt{bump(5)} and \texttt{bump(4)}, and \texttt{bump}
returns its argument plus one. An interval analysis without calling contexts must describe both calls at
the return of \texttt{bump} and therefore cannot prove either check. With
contexts that distinguish the arguments, it analyzes \texttt{bump} separately
for each argument and proves both. Goblint, an abstract
interpreter for C~\cite{vojdani16,seidl26}, supports this form of context sensitivity through a \emph{side-effecting constraint
system}~\cite{seidl03,apinis12}. Its inequations relate unknowns
indexed by program point and context, and the right-hand side of one unknown
may also contribute to other unknowns. A generic top-down solver then solves the
system. A defect in any of these layers can produce an unsound verdict.
\Cref{sec:evaluation} examines one such defect in Goblint's remainder operation.

Stade et al.\ proved the solver partially correct in
Isabelle/HOL~\cite{stade24}, and Tilscher et al.\ extended the proof to side
effects~\cite{tilscher26}. When the solver terminates, its valuation satisfies
the inequations for all solved unknowns: it is a \emph{post-solution} on that
set. The solved set is closed under the reads of its right-hand sides.
The solver theorem does not establish that these equations soundly
approximate program execution. To prove this, we need a concrete semantics
that associates reachable stores with calling contexts. Such semantics are established in the
literature~\cite{sharir81,knoop92,jeannet04,vanhorn10} (\cref{sec:related}). To
our knowledge, no mechanization connects a source semantics through such a context-indexed
semantics to a side-effecting constraint system and its verified solver.

We mechanize this connection in Voblint (verified Goblint), an Isabelle/HOL
formalization with the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We adapt the local traces of Schwarz et al.~\cite{schwarz21} to
    procedure activations. A context policy, which may admit several contexts
    for one call, relates these activation traces to contexts. For an arbitrary
    control-flow graph, we give five local obligations on a cover and prove
    that any cover satisfying them bounds the final store of every valid trace
    in every admitted context (\cref{sec:traces}).
  \item We prove that every post-solution of the generated, context-routed
    equations meets these obligations, provided the context policy agrees with
    the routing of the equations. The verified solver enters only through its
    certificate (\cref{sec:equations}).
  \item We prove an end-to-end theorem about the analyzer function that
    Isabelle exports to OCaml. For every accepted configuration, whenever the
    analyzer returns a report, the report covers every store reached by a finite source
    execution at its program point. Every definite verdict there holds for
    those stores (\cref{sec:theorem}).
  \item We demonstrate concrete instances of the theorem, show that the faulty
    remainder operation behind a Goblint defect violates a domain obligation,
    and compare the verdicts obtained with different calling contexts on
    recursive programs (\cref{sec:evaluation}).
\end{itemize}
The solver, side-effecting constraint systems, local traces, and Goblint's
separation of analyses and solvers are prior work. Our proof uses
context-indexed collecting semantics. The main end-to-end theorem combines
the reported states across contexts at each program point. A companion
theorem also establishes coverage by the reported states for each individual
context (\cref{sec:theorem}).

VIMP supports local and global integer variables and recursive procedures with
parameters and return values, but no pointers, arrays, or heap. The correctness
results are conditional on solver termination, which is not proved for every
program. The parser and report printer are unverified handwritten code;
Isabelle's code generator, the OCaml toolchain, and the solver-tracing hook
used in traced runs are trusted.
